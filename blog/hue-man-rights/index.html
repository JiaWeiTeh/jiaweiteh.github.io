Hue‑man Rights in Data Visualisation

How colour theory and colour-blind friendliness shade your data.

Jia Wei Teh · October 2026 · Adapted from a talk

This is the written version of a talk I give on colour in scientific figures. There are three parts: how we see colour (and how we don’t), how colourmaps can lie to us, and when it’s fine to break the rules.

A disclaimer first: I am not a medical doctor. This is a look at colour from the physics and perception side, not the medical one.

1. How we see colour

Our eyes have three types of cone cells, sensitive to different parts of the visible spectrum. Long-wavelength (L) cones respond most strongly to yellow-green light, medium-wavelength (M) cones to green, and short-wavelength (S) cones to blue-violet. Their sensitivities overlap quite a lot, and the colours we see depend on their relative responses.

Your retina does colour maths

Because the cone sensitivities overlap, their signals contain a lot of redundant information. Rather than simply passing along three independent measurements, our visual system compares them.

To a first approximation, these comparisons give us three channels: brightness (L + M), red–green (L − M), and blue–yellow (S − (L + M)). This is called opponent colour processing, and it helps explain why we can imagine a reddish yellow or a bluish green, but not a colour that is simultaneously reddish and greenish, or yellowish and blue (Hering 1878; Hurvich & Jameson 1957).

In other words, your retina is already doing a bit of data processing before the signal even reaches your brain.

Colour isn’t universal

Even among people with normal colour vision, we don’t all see or name colours in quite the same way.

In 2010, xkcd ran an online colour survey with more than 200,000 participants. People were shown colours and asked to name them. The results are a rather entertaining reminder that we don’t always agree on what counts as blue, green or purple. (And that humanity, apparently, can’t spell fuchsia.)

We aren’t equally good at telling all colours apart, either.

In 1942, David MacAdam measured how much a colour could change before people noticed the difference. His results are often shown as ellipses on a chromaticity diagram. Within each ellipse, the colours are difficult to distinguish.

The interesting part is that the ellipses aren’t all the same size or shape. On this diagram, some of the largest are in the green region, while some of the smallest are in the blue. Equal distances on the diagram clearly don’t correspond to equal differences in what we see.

Colour-blindness in a nutshell

Now imagine what happens when one of the three cone systems is missing or doesn’t work normally.

In protanopia, the long-wavelength cone system is absent. In deuteranopia, it’s the medium-wavelength system. Both affect the ability to distinguish certain reds and greens. Tritanopia, which affects the short-wavelength system, causes confusion between some blues and yellows.

There are also milder forms where all three cone types are present, but their sensitivities differ from those of people with typical colour vision.

Why should we care?

Colour-vision deficiency is not particularly rare. Commonly quoted estimates are about 8% of men and 0.5% of women, especially in populations of Northern European ancestry.

That works out to roughly 16 in every 200 men and one in every 200 women. One of them might be your supervisor, a colleague, or the reviewer deciding whether your paper gets published.

And if humans aren’t enough to convince you, consider the dogs. All 100 out of 100 dogs have dichromatic colour vision, so they can’t distinguish reds and greens the way most humans can. They don’t see the world in black and white, though.

So: use colour-blind-friendly palettes, and check them

Fortunately, this isn’t difficult to address.

There are plenty of colour-blind-friendly palettes available. CMasher, for example, provides a collection of colourmaps together with simulations of how they appear to viewers with different colour-vision deficiencies.

Before submitting a paper or giving a talk, it’s worth spending a minute checking your figures with a simulator such as Coblis.

Take-home 1: We don’t all see colours the same way. Make your figures accessible.

And take-home 1.5: some mantis shrimp have 12 types of colour photoreceptor. Strangely, that doesn’t mean they are better at distinguishing colours than we are. Still, imagine if their scientific figures only worked for shrimps.

2. When colourmaps lie

Our eyes don’t work linearly

We don’t perceive brightness in the same way that a light meter measures intensity.

A change from one unit of light to two can be much more noticeable than a change from 100 to 101. What matters is often the relative change, not just the absolute difference. This is related to Weber’s law and the approximately logarithmic relationship between stimulus intensity and perception described by Fechner.

Of course, human vision is more complicated than a single logarithm. Our eyes also adapt to different lighting conditions, allowing us to see across an enormous range of brightness.

This matters when choosing colourmaps because equal numerical changes don’t automatically look like equal changes in brightness.

Perceptual uniformity

Ideally, a colourmap should be perceptually uniform. If the data changes by the same amount twice, the corresponding changes in colour should look roughly equally large.

The very popular jet colourmap is not particularly good at this.

Try printing jet in greyscale. Instead of getting a smooth progression from dark to light, you’ll see the brightness rise and fall. Some regions look much brighter than their neighbours, creating apparent bands and boundaries that aren’t necessarily present in the data.

We can measure this more carefully by treating colours as points in a colour space and calculating the distance between neighbouring points.

The choice of colour space matters. A simple distance between RGB values doesn’t tell us very much about perceived differences, because RGB wasn’t designed to be perceptually uniform.

A better approximation is CIELAB, where colours are described by lightness (L*) and two colour coordinates (a* and b*). The distance between two colours is then

ΔE*ab = √[(ΔL*)² + (Δa*)² + (Δb*)²]

CIELAB is not perfectly perceptually uniform, but it is considerably more useful for this purpose than raw RGB distances. More advanced spaces, such as CAM02-UCS, can also be used.

The idea is simple: for a perceptually uniform colourmap, neighbouring colours should be separated by roughly equal perceptual distances.

The viscm tool lets you examine this for different colourmaps. For jet, the perceptual derivative varies considerably, its lightness rises and falls, and some colours become difficult to distinguish under simulated red–green colour blindness.

The face test

Here’s a less technical way to see the problem.

Take a familiar photograph and apply different colourmaps to it. With a reasonably uniform colourmap, the shading and structure of the face remain recognisable. With jet, some features become exaggerated while others nearly disappear.

It’s not a rigorous scientific test, but it’s a rather good way to convince someone that the choice of colourmap matters.

Colour qualifies, the legend quantifies

Colour is useful for showing patterns and trends. But the actual numbers belong on the colourbar.

For an example, look at the rainfall map of Texas in the European Commission’s Data Visualisation Guide. Without the legend, it’s difficult to tell which areas are wetter. Is purple wetter than yellow? What about green?

A sequential colourmap, where the brightness changes steadily, makes the ordering much easier to see. You still need the colourbar to read the values, but at least you can recognise which regions have more or less rainfall.

And if the figure still makes sense when printed in greyscale, that’s usually a good sign.

Normalise your data

Sometimes the problem isn’t even the colourmap. It’s what you’re choosing to colour.

Take the familiar county-level map of the 2016 US presidential election. Most of the map looks red because a lot of counties covering large areas voted Republican.

But land doesn’t vote. People do.

In his animation “Land doesn’t vote, people do”, Karim Douïeb replaces the geographical areas with circles whose sizes represent the number of votes. The picture changes quite dramatically.

The original map isn’t necessarily wrong. It shows which candidate won each county. But if you’re interested in how people voted, you need a representation that accounts for how many voters live in each place.

This is a useful reminder that the right colourmap can’t rescue a figure that represents the wrong quantity.

A case study: ozone

This is one of my favourite examples of how a colourmap can change the interpretation of real scientific data.

The figure shows total column ozone in the Northern Hemisphere, plotted with a rainbow colourmap.

Looking at it, you might identify three regions where the ozone concentration changes sharply with latitude: a polar front, a mid-latitude front and a sub-polar front.

Now plot the same data with a more uniform colour scale. Better still, calculate the gradient of the ozone field to see where the changes are actually largest.

The red and yellow fronts are still there. But the blue front is much less convincing. What looked like a sharp boundary was largely an artefact of the rainbow colourmap.

This is the problem with non-uniform colourmaps. A narrow range of values can occupy a visually striking part of the colour scale, making a gradual change look abrupt.

If the apparent feature is caused by your choice of colourmap rather than the data, it shouldn’t be used to draw a physical conclusion.

The solution

Fortunately, matplotlib already includes several good alternatives: inferno, magma, plasma and viridis.

These colourmaps were designed to be approximately perceptually uniform, with steadily increasing lightness. They generally work well in greyscale and remain reasonably readable for many viewers with colour-vision deficiencies.

They aren’t the only good choices, of course. Diverging colourmaps are useful when your data have a meaningful central value, and cyclic colourmaps are better for quantities such as angles or phases.

The important thing is to choose a colourmap that suits the data rather than using the default without thinking.

Take-home 2: Equal changes in data should look like equal changes in colour. Don’t let your colourmap invent features.

3. Breaking the rules

Every cloud has a silver lining

So, should we ban rainbow colourmaps altogether?

Not necessarily.

Their strong changes in hue and brightness can make small details easier to spot, even if those changes aren’t proportional to the underlying data. This can be useful when the goal is to explore a complex image rather than measure values precisely.

Google’s Turbo colourmap was designed with this in mind. It keeps much of the high contrast of jet while reducing some of its more obvious problems, such as artificial banding.

In Anton Mikhailov’s introduction to Turbo (2019), there are examples of depth images where the additional contrast helps distinguish features.

Turbo is still not perceptually uniform, and its lightness is not monotonic, so it isn’t a universal replacement for viridis. But it shows that there can be reasonable trade-offs between quantitative accuracy and visual detail, depending on what the figure is meant to do.

The upside of inaccurate maps

Sometimes you want a figure to reveal faint structures or help you notice something that might otherwise be missed.

High-contrast colourmaps can be useful for this, including in some medical imaging applications. For an example of PET image visualisation, see Burgos et al. (2014).

But there is an important distinction between using an image to spot something and using it to measure something.

A colourmap that helps you notice a faint feature isn’t necessarily the best one for judging its amplitude. If you are making a quantitative claim, you should check that the feature survives a more appropriate representation of the data.

A thought experiment

You have probably seen Ishihara colour-vision tests: plates made of coloured dots, with a number hidden inside.

People with different types of colour vision may see different numbers, or no number at all.

xkcd took this idea a little further in its Combination Vision Test.

It’s a funny example, but also a useful final reminder: a figure that looks perfectly obvious to you might not be obvious to someone else.

So use perceptually uniform colourmaps when the numbers matter, make your figures accessible, and don’t be afraid to break the rules when you have a good reason.

And please, fix your defaults.
